# Chosen, Internalized and Imposed Goals

## Operational definition

Goal autonomy distinguishes goals a person endorses from goals experienced
mainly as external pressure. Internalization can be partial: a required activity
may contain personally valued reasons without becoming intrinsically enjoyable.

## What this concept does not mean

It does not reduce motivation to an intrinsic-versus-extrinsic binary, imply
that obligations are unreal, or require manufacturing enthusiasm for every
goal. External consequences and duties can remain important facts.

## Main mechanisms

Perceived choice, competence and relatedness are proposed to support more
self-determined regulation. Pressure, unclear value and low perceived capacity
can produce different patterns even when outward behavior looks similar.

## Evidence summary

A meta-analysis of randomized health-behavior trials found small average effects
for self-determination-theory interventions and reported evidence consistent
with theoretically specified mediation, alongside publication and small-sample
bias. A separate review linked need-supportive intervention strategies with
autonomy support, need satisfaction and motivation; individual strategies had
limited independent associations. These aggregate findings do not establish why
one person rejects or maintains a specific goal.

## Evidence mapping

- Self-determination-theory interventions showed aggregate effects across randomized trials: `src-sdt-rct-2020` (`meta_analysis`).
- Need-supportive strategies were associated with autonomy support, need satisfaction and motivation across interventions: `src-sdt-techniques-2019` (`meta_analysis`).

## Relevant signals

The person says the goal belongs to someone else, reports pressure as the main
reason for action, no longer sees value in the objective, or wants to stop
despite an otherwise feasible plan.

## Alternative explanations

Apparent low motivation may reflect insufficient time, sleep, skill, access,
pain, unclear requirements or an unrealistic plan. Refusing one strategy does
not prove rejection of the underlying goal.

## Information needed

Who established the goal, actual consequences of refusal, personally endorsed
value, available choice, possibilities for negotiation and costs to the person
and others.

## Practical implications

Separate non-negotiable constraints from genuine choice. The relevant decision
may be to abandon the objective, change its reason or scope, or revise the plan
while preserving a personally valued outcome.

## Limitations

This framework does not adjudicate legal, employment, educational or care
obligations. Aggregate intervention effects should not be presented as a causal
explanation for an individual's motivation.
